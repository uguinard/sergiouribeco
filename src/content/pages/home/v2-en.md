I teach Spanish by letting students live it, not by testing whether they can recite it.

When I walk into a Year 7 class in Bangkok, or a Grade 10 group in Colombia, or an IB ab initio room in Phuket, the first thing I do is meet students where their world already lives. That world might be a conversation about street food, a photo from home, a confusion about a word that sounds like something else in their first language, or a question that has nothing to do with the textbook. I start there. Because language is not a subject to memorise — it is a way to move through the world with other people.

That is what drew me to Alma Forest School. Not the credential. The idea that a school can be organised around how children actually learn: slowly, through relationship, through repeated meaningful contact, through the kind of natural induction that happens when adults treat them as capable observers and participants, not empty vessels to fill. I want to teach in a place that believes a forest floor can be a classroom; that a child asking about moss is doing the same cognitive work as a question about grammar; and that the community formed around those moments is, in the end, the lesson that stays.

My work is grounded in comprehensible input — not as a technique, but as a commitment. I arrange the classroom so that Spanish enters at the edge of what each student already understands, and I keep it there long enough for acquisition to happen without anxiety. I gesture, I pause, I repeat meaning, not form. I build lessons around story because story is the oldest i+1 delivery system we have. And I design assessments that measure whether a student can use the language, not whether they can conjugate under pressure.

That approach has two roots in my experience. One is classroom practice: four years teaching IB Spanish in Colombia, Turkey, and Thailand, plus a developed methodology called NeuroSpanish that ties sensory, embodied, and narrative pathways together so new words land in more than one part of the mind at once. The other is unlearning. Every year I drop another habit that inherited methods taught me — grade-for-effort, speed over accuracy, the belief that English is the safest place to stay. Unlearning those defaults is what keeps the work honest. It is also what makes it possible to teach a thirteen-year-old who wants nothing to do with school, or a sixteen-year-old who thinks they are “not a language person.” I know that label is wrong. I know it because I have seen it dissolve, usually around the third or fourth story.

Outside the classroom I build resources: H5P activities, AI-generated input pipelines, scope-and-sequence documents that start from the learner, not from the examination. I present at department level on feedback design and formative assessment. I co-advise and tutor pastoral care because learning does not happen in a vacuum. I speak Spanish, English, and French, and I know what it costs to cross between them.

But none of that is the headline. The headline is this: I am a teacher who believes language belongs to the people who speak it, and that a school’s job is to hand it over gently. I am applying to Alma Forest because it seems to believe the same.
