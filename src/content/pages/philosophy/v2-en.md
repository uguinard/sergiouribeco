The place where I start is not the textbook. It is people arriving, each with a different conceptual world already in place, meeting Spanish.

## What I mean by language

A language is not a lookup table for an experience that is the same for everyone. It is a particular way of shaping attention. In English we notice time as a sequence of countable units. In Spanish we encounter the imperfect as a doorway into felt duration, the personal *a* as a grammatical gesture toward personhood, words like *sobremesa* or *añoranza* that name whole textures of social and emotional life. Learning Spanish is not adding labels to an existing world. It is letting that world begin to rearrange itself. That is why intercultural empathy is not tolerance of difference. It is perceptual access to difference — the kind of access that only a language can provide.

## How I think learning happens

I work from three converging ideas that show up in the classroom every day.

**Natural induction.** Children do not wait for a syllabus to tell them what matters. They notice what repeats, what is meaningful, what is connected to action and relationship. My job is to make the repeated exposure coherent and rich enough that the induction can carry them across the bridge between languages.

**Contextual natural induction.** Meaning does not arrive disembodied. A word about food should be experienced where food is smelled and shared. A word about movement should be experienced while moving. The classroom becomes a place where language is continually embedded in the contexts that give it reason to exist.

**Comprehensible input at i+1.** Stephen Krashen’s core insight is operational, not abstract: acquisition happens at the edge of current competence, where the learner can understand but must stretch. I spend more time protecting that stretch than I spend correcting errors. Errors are the weather of i+1; they are not the destination.

## Why I bring emotion into it

Antonio Damasio showed that reason and feeling are not opponents. That finding matters in Year 7 and in Year 12. A student who is anxious, who fears looking foolish, whose identity feels under negotiation, will not acquire. They might comply. Compliance is not acquisition. My work is partly pastoral: I design low-stakes entry points, high-repetition rituals, and story sequences that let students meet Spanish sideways, through characters and situations, before they are asked to perform it themselves.

## What embodiment adds

I describe what I do as **NeuroSpanish** — not as a brand, but as a label for a method that ties gesture, sensory context, cultural reference, and narrative scene to the same lexical item. From embodied, embedded, enacted, and extended cognition I take the idea that knowing is not in the head alone. It is distributed across the body, the room, and the community. SENSUM — the multisensory scaffold I developed — is how I translate that distribution into something you can plan and run in a fifty-minute lesson.

## What stays

When the unit ends, what should remain is not a list of irregular past-tense forms. It is a student who speaks Spanish without switching languages first to check whether they are allowed. It is a student who volunteers a sentence because the meaning needs to leave their body, not because it will be marked. It is a student who later meets a Spanish-speaking peer and treats that meeting as a continuation of something already familiar.

That is the classroom I keep trying to build. Every year I unlearn another habit that would get in the way.
